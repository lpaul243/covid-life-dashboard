What this dashboard asks

What happened to jobs, prices, paychecks, home prices, and deaths in the United States from 2014 to 2024, and how did the COVID years change things? Each panel answers one question.

How to read a panel

1. The question is in bold at the top of each panel.

2. Where and when tells you the place (U.S. or states) and the years.

3. The chart shows the data. Hover over it, or tap on a phone, to see exact values.

4. What it shows explains the chart in plain words.

5. Limits says what the chart cannot tell you.

Grey shading on a chart marks the COVID period.

Words used here

Births minus deaths
The number of births in a year minus the number of deaths that year. A positive number means more people were born than died.
Deaths per 100,000
How many people died per 100,000 residents. This lets places of different sizes be compared fairly.
Crude (raw) rate
The plain count of deaths divided by population, with no adjustment for age. Compare it with care, because older places can have higher rates for reasons unrelated to COVID.
Median household income
The income of the middle household: half earn more, half earn less.
Real (or 2025 dollars)
Dollar amounts adjusted for inflation, so $82,750 in 2019 and $82,117 in 2023 can be compared directly.
Inflation
How much prices rose over a year, as a percentage.
Unemployment rate
The share of people who want a job and are actively looking, but don't have one.
Labor force participation
The share of adults who are working or looking for work.
Home price index
A number that tracks home prices over time. Compare how much it changed, not its size.
Correlation
A score from -1 to +1 showing whether two measures move together across places. Near 0 means no clear straight-line pattern. It does not prove that one causes the other.

1. Did jobs disappear in 2020, and did they come back?

Where: U.S. (national) · When: monthly, January 2019 to December 2021

What it shows

Unemployment was about 3.5% before COVID. It jumped to 14.8% in April 2020, the highest month in this data. By December 2021 it was back to 3.9%.

Limits

This counts people actively looking for work. It does not count people who stopped looking. It is also national only.

2. Did births keep up with deaths?

Where: U.S. (national) · When: yearly, 2014 to 2024

The line is births minus deaths: how many more people were born than died. The bars are COVID deaths.

What it shows

Births minus deaths fell from about 1.36 million in 2014 to about 200,000 in 2021, as deaths rose. COVID caused about 351,000 deaths in 2020 and about 417,000 in 2021. Births also fell steadily, from 3.99 million in 2014 to 3.60 million in 2023.

Limits

COVID deaths are part of total deaths, not on top of them. The chart does not show how many of those people would have died of something else that year.

How to read a dot chart: each dot is a state. The dots sit at their values on the two axes. If they form a rising or falling line, the two measures move together. If they scatter in a cloud, they don't. The correlation number sums this up from -1 to +1, where 0 means no clear pattern. A cloud does not prove that no link exists. It only means this data does not show one.

Suicide and unemployment, month by month

Where: U.S. (national) · When: monthly, January 2019 to December 2021

The solid line is unemployment (left axis). The dashed line is suicide deaths per month (right axis).

What it shows

Suicide deaths dipped in April 2020, the month unemployment peaked, and then rose again while unemployment was still high. The two lines do not move together in a clear way.

Limits

Lockdowns, layoffs, and stimulus payments happened at the same time, so this chart cannot separate their effects. Recent months may be incomplete.

Missing years and incomplete values

  • 2020 household income: the Census did not publish a 1-year estimate, so that year is blank.
  • State COVID counts for 2024 and 2025: incomplete, so the state map stops at 2023. National counts run through 2024.
  • Withheld counts: the CDC hides counts from 1 to 9, and the Census marks some business counts as withheld. Totals can be understated where that happens.
  • Inflation for 2025: left out, because the year was not complete.
  • Business counts for 2017: the file was not included.

Level of detail

  • National only: the jobs, births, inflation, income, home price, and top-causes panels are national.
  • Yearly averages: state unemployment and labor force values are yearly averages, so a short spike can be averaged away.
  • Counts, not rates: the top-causes panel shows how many people died, not how often, so population growth can affect it.
  • Crude (raw) COVID rates: the map and rankings do not adjust for age, so older states can look worse.

Changes in definitions

  • Business industry codes changed in 2017, so counts before and after may shift slightly.
  • Cause-of-death list: COVID-19 was added as a cause in 2020.
  • Home prices are FHFA's developmental index. Values can be revised when new data arrives.
  • Cost of living (BEA price index) is available only through 2024.

What the data cannot tell us

  • Correlation does not show cause. Stimulus payments and expanded unemployment benefits in 2020 and 2021 may have supported incomes, which would weaken any link between job losses and income.
  • Suicide is one measure of mental health, not the whole picture.
  • Death counts use the underlying cause only. A death with COVID as a contributing cause is counted under its underlying cause.

Sources

  • Unemployment, labor force, and labor force participation: Bureau of Labor Statistics, Local Area Unemployment Statistics, annual averages by state, and monthly national unemployment (FRED series UNRATE).
  • COVID deaths by state: CDC National Center for Health Statistics, provisional counts by state of residence.
  • Causes of death, births, and all deaths, national: CDC WONDER, Underlying Cause of Death (1999 to 2020 and 2018 to 2024 single-race files, ICD-10 113 cause list), and Natality.
  • Suicide deaths, monthly: CDC WONDER, Underlying Cause of Death.
  • Home prices: Federal Housing Finance Agency, House Price Index, All-Transactions, annual state and national data (last updated March 31, 2026).
  • Median household income: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 1-year estimates, table B19013.
  • Personal income and population: Bureau of Economic Analysis, SAINC1, and Census population estimates.
  • Cost of living: Bureau of Economic Analysis, Regional Price Parities, all items.
  • Inflation: Bureau of Labor Statistics, Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), annual averages.
  • Hospitals, funeral homes, and cemeteries: Census Bureau, County Business Patterns, NAICS 622, 812210, and 812220.
  • Population by race: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 1-year estimates, table B03002.

Method notes

  • Correlations are Pearson correlations across the 50 states.
  • Purchasing power = real median income divided by (price index ÷ 100).
  • Inflation for a year = change in the annual average CPI from the year before.
  • Size groups use quartiles of the 2025 population age 16 and over.
  • Washington, D.C. is excluded from state charts, to match the other files.
